### LM-01 ★: The Elo rating system treats each answer as a match between learner skill and item difficulty. It needs no population fitting, and an uncertainty function that shrinks the update size makes it usable from the first answer.

- **Status:** `verified` · Tier 2 · <https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/CAE-elo.pdf>
- **Verification:** **corrected**. Correction: Pelánek (Computers & Education 2016, 'Applications of the Elo Rating System in Adaptive Educational Systems') models each answer as a match between learner skill and item difficulty. It uses online heuristic updates (s += K(correct-P), d += K(P-correct), P = 1/(1+e^-(s-d)), initial values 0) with no batch or maximum-likelihood fitting. A shifted logistic, 1/k + (1-1/k)/(1+e^-(s-d)), handles k-option multiple choice. Earlier work used K=0.4. The uncertainty function U(n)=a/(1+bn) gives fast coarse estimates that are then fine-tuned. The paper fitted a=1, b=0.05 by grid search on geography data and a=4, b=0.5 on simulated data, and says many choices of a and b give very similar results. It suggests separate uncertainty functions for items and students. The Outline Maps system targets a success rate of, for example, 75%. The paper calls Elo 'simple, robust, and effective'. Caveat: item-difficulty estimates still come from many learners' answers, and the meta-parameters were fitted to population data, so 'needs no population fitting' overstates the source. (`observed`). Note: I extracted the full PDF text and checked every number and quote: update rules (sec 2.2), initial values 0, shifted logistic, K=0.4 (Antal 2013; Wauters 2012), U(n)=a/(1+bn) with a=1, b=0.05 (Papousek 2014, Niznan 2015, geography case study: 'many different choices of a, b provide very similar results'), a=4, b=0.5 for simulated data, separate item and student uncertainty functions (sec 2.3), the 75% target (sec 4.1), and the 'simple, robust, and effective' quote from the abstract. Two corrections. First, 'needs no population fitting' is the researcher's inference: Elo avoids batch fitting, but difficulties are learned from many students and a and b were grid-searched on data. For a single user, item difficulties will not be estimated well from one learner's answers. Second, epistemic 'verified' is reserved for official Brilliant sources, so this academic paper is 'observed'. Its source_tier of 2 refers to non-Brilliant literature.
- **Detail:** Update rules: theta_s += K*(correct - P), d_i += K*(P - correct), with P = logistic(theta_s - d_i). Initial values are 0. For multiple choice, a shifted logistic adds a guessing floor. Constant K=0.4 was used in earlier work. The uncertainty function is U(n)=a/(1+b*n): a=1, b=0.05 when fitted on geography facts, a=4, b=0.5 on simulated data. The paper says many choices of a and b give very similar results and suggests separate uncertainty functions for items and learners. Its Outline Maps system targets a 75% success rate when it selects questions. The author calls Elo 'simple, robust, and effective'.

### LM-02 ★: Elo has two extensions that suit a concept graph. A multivariate version updates several related skills in proportion to item-skill weights, and PFAE uses separate step sizes for correct and incorrect answers so that learning is modeled, not just assessed.

- **Status:** `verified` · Tier 2 · <https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/CAE-elo.pdf>
- **Verification:** **corrected**. Correction: Pelánek (2016) describes two Elo extensions. (1) A multivariate extension: after an answer on knowledge component i, every skill j is updated by s_j += c_ij*K*(correct-P), where c_ij are correlations between knowledge components computed from data, not item-skill weights. Earlier applications combined it with an uncertainty function and a global skill. (2) PFAE ('Performance Factor Analysis Extended / Elo'): s += gamma*(1-P) after a correct answer and s += delta*P after an incorrect one. It uses different constants because an answer is an opportunity to learn as well as evidence, and it can be viewed as an extension of PFA. The paper explicitly warns that complex domains with prerequisite relations among skills need more involved modeling or a novel Elo extension. Whether these extensions 'suit a concept graph' is therefore inference, and the source itself cautions against it. (`observed`). Note: Formulas checked in the extracted PDF text (sec 2.4, 2.5, 5.x). Corrections: (a) the c_ij are KC-to-KC correlations ('Based on available data we compute correlations cij between knowledge components'), not item-skill weights; (b) the paper prints the incorrect-answer update as s + delta*P and never states delta's sign. A negative delta is implied (and used in the original Papousek work), but that is inference. (c) The headline 'suit a concept graph' is the researcher's inference and conflicts with the paper's own warning about prerequisite-structured domains. (d) Epistemic should be observed, not verified (not a Brilliant source).
- **Detail:** Multivariate: theta_sj += c_ij*K*(correct - P). It is usually combined with a global skill and an uncertainty function. PFAE: theta += gamma*(1-P) after a correct answer and theta += delta*P after an incorrect one (delta is applied as a negative step); it is framed as an extension of Performance Factors Analysis. Pelánek warns that complex domains with prerequisite relations among skills need more involved modeling or a new Elo extension.

### LM-03 ★: If item difficulties are updated in the same loop as learner ability while items are chosen adaptively, the ratings drift and never converge.

- **Status:** `verified` · Tier 2 · <https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/keeping-elo-alive-evaluating-and-improving-measurement-properties/>
- **Verification:** **corrected**. Correction: Bolsinova, Gergely and Brinkhuis (2025, British Journal of Mathematical and Statistical Psychology, doi 10.1111/bmsp.12395) ran a simulation study. When items are selected adaptively from current ratings and item difficulties are updated together with student abilities, rating variance across items and students increases artificially over time and the ratings do not converge. They also find Elo ratings are generally biased, with variance that depends on context. Their proposed fix is two parallel chains of ratings, which removes the dependence of item selection on current rating errors. (`observed`). Note: The Tilburg research portal and the PMC/Tilburg repository abstract (seen in search) confirm all three points: artificial variance increase, non-convergence under adaptive selection with simultaneous updating, and two parallel chains. Content is accurate. 'For a single user this argues for fixing item difficulty from generator features' is the researcher's inference, not the paper's. Epistemic corrected from verified to observed (peer-reviewed paper, not Brilliant).
- **Detail:** Bolsinova, Gergely and Brinkhuis (2025, Br. J. Math. Stat. Psychol.) report that rating variance increases artificially over time and ratings do not converge under adaptive selection. Their fix is two parallel chains of ratings, so that item selection does not depend on current rating errors. For a single user this argues for fixing item difficulty from generator features rather than co-estimating it.

### LM-06 ★: BKT parameters need bounds to avoid degenerate models. The bounds commonly cited from the literature are P(G)<0.3 and P(S)<0.1 (Corbett and Anderson 1995), P(G)<0.5 and P(S)<0.5 (Baker, Corbett and Aleven 2008), and P(G)+P(S)<1 (Beck).

- **Status:** `observed` · Tier 7 · <https://yilinl.quarto.pub/bayesian-knowledge-tracing>
- **Verification:** **confirmed**. Note: The quarto page lists exactly Corbett & Anderson (1995) P(G)<0.3, P(S)<0.1; Baker, Corbett & Aleven (2008) P(G)<0.5, P(S)<0.5; Beck P(G)+P(S)<1.0. It defines degeneracy as knowing a skill leading to worse performance, or getting it wrong implying knowing it, and gives the four parameters. The page names no author. I found a better, more primary source: Ryan Baker's Big Data and Education MOOC slides, 'Week 4 Video 2 - Knowledge Inference: Bayesian Knowledge Tracing' (https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/MOOT/slides/W004V002.pdf). Its 'Parameter Constraints' and 'Constraints Proposed' slides contain the same three bounds and the same degeneracy definition. This confirms the bounds come from Baker's lecture material, not the original papers. Observed is the right epistemic. Tier could be raised from 7 to the slides (instructor lecture material). The original 1995 and 2008 papers were still not checked directly.
- **Detail:** A degenerate model is one where knowing a skill predicts worse performance, or where getting a skill wrong implies knowing it. BKT has four parameters: P(L0), P(T), P(G), P(S). These bounds come from course notes summarizing Baker's knowledge-tracing lectures, not from the original papers, which I could not retrieve. If BKT is used with expert priors in a single-user app, these bounds are the natural constraints.

### LM-07 ★: The usual 'BKT P(L)>0.95 means mastered' rule tends to declare mastery too early when the data do not match BKT's binary assumptions. A simple exponential moving average, with the degree of knowledge kept separate from the uncertainty of the estimate, is a pragmatic alternative.

- **Status:** `verified` · Tier 2 · <https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/mastery-modeling.pdf>
- **Verification:** **corrected**. N-consecutive-correct performs well when observations have low noise but has 'limited memory', resetting after any error. EMA (theta_k = alpha*theta_{k-1} + (1-alpha)*c_k, mastered when theta_k >= T) can closely imitate the paper's LogisticHMM; decision correlation is often over 0.9. In that one simulated example, the best-fit alpha was 0.85-0.95 and T was 0.65-0.97, depending on the required knowledge state and certainty. The paper notes that EMA's parameters lack clear interpretation and are hard to set. Its separate pragmatic recommendation is a simple approach that explicitly separates estimated degree of knowledge from estimate uncertainty, for example by tracking 'average performance' and 'number of attempts'. (`observed`). Note: Full text extracted and checked (sec 3.1-3.3, 4.1, Table 1). The numbers and quotes are accurate. Corrections: (a) the BKT finding and the alpha and T ranges come from a single simulated running example, not empirical data, so they are not general best-fit values. - **Detail:** Pelánek, 'Conceptual Issues in Mastery Criteria': BKT fitted to non-binary learning data produces inflated guess and slip values and 'nearly always declares mastery too early'. ### LM-08 ★: Mastery decisions depend more on which data feed them and where the thresholds sit than on which learner model is used. In ASSISTments data, 3 consecutive correct answers gave adequate practice, and higher N better predicted transfer.

- **Status:** `observed` · Tier 2 · <https://www.ics.muni.cz/en/research/publications/1392193>
- **Verification:** **confirmed**. Note: Pelánek & Řihák, 'Experimental Analysis of Mastery Learning Criteria', UMAP 2017 (doi 10.1145/3079628.3079667). The ICS MUNI abstract says data sources and thresholds are 'more important than the choice of a learner modeling technique' and that 'a simple exponential moving average method is a suitable technique'. I fetched and read the full Kelly, Wang, Thompson & Heffernan EDM 2015 poster. Its abstract says N-CCR is more accurate than KT, '3 consecutive correct responses provides adequate practice, especially for students who reach the threshold without making an error', and higher N-CCR thresholds predict transfer better than lower N-CCR or KT. Nuances to carry forward: for students with no errors, KT was 'comparable to 3-CCR' on next-problem correctness. The transfer result comes from a small randomized controlled trial (about 37 students in the tables). In that trial 5-CCR beat 3-CCR, and KT was comparable to 3-CCR. Observed is the right epistemic.
- **Detail:** Pelánek and Řihák (UMAP 2017): choosing data sources and thresholds matters more than choosing the learner-modeling technique, and EMA is a suitable criterion. Three in a row was adequate, especially for students who reached it without errors. Higher N predicted transfer-question performance better. Both are from abstracts seen in search results; I did not read the full texts.

### LM-09: Performance Factors Analysis and the Logistic Knowledge Tracing framework that generalizes it model correctness as a logistic function of per-skill counts of prior successes and failures. Unlike BKT, they naturally handle items that involve several skills.

- **Status:** `observed` · Tier 2 · <https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.00869>
- **Detail:** PFA (Pavlik, Cen and Koedinger, AIED 2009) counts successes and failures separately; AFM counts all attempts. LKT (Pavlik, Eglington, Harrell-Williams) adds features such as recency (time since the skill was last practiced) and R-PFA, a recency-weighted proportion correct seeded with 'ghost' attempts (for example 1 ghost success and 1 ghost failure). Ghost attempts act as priors when data are scarce.

### LM-10: Dynamic Bayesian networks that encode a hierarchy of prerequisite skills predicted better than per-skill BKT across math, spelling and physics datasets, but their parameter count grows exponentially with the number of prerequisite links.

### SR-04 ★: Math Academy's FIRe algorithm extends spaced repetition to a knowledge graph. A successful review of an advanced topic gives discounted implicit credit to the topics it encompasses, failures propagate upward as penalties, and tasks are chosen so their implicit credit clears other due reviews.

- **Status:** `verified` · Tier 2 · <https://www.justinmath.com/individualized-spaced-repetition-in-hierarchical-knowledge-structures/>
- **Verification:** **corrected**. Correction: Justin Skycak's blog post (Oct 5 2023; he built the system at Math Academy) describes Fractional Implicit Repetition (FIRe). Encompassing weights are fractions, interpreted as the probability that a random advanced-topic problem encompasses a random simpler-topic problem. Credit flows downward: undiminished along full encompassings, fading along partial ones. Implicit credit (rawDelta) is discounted when a repetition comes early relative to the desired interval. Failure penalties travel upward. The updates are repNum = max(0, repNum + speed*decay^failed*rawDelta) and memory = max(0, memory + rawDelta)*0.5^(days/interval). decay starts at 1 and grows as the repetition becomes more overdue. speed = (speedup from student ability)/(slowdown from topic difficulty), and when speed < 1 incoming implicit credit is discarded in favor of explicit reviews. Domain experts set nontrivial weights, prioritizing close prerequisite edges that repetition flow cannot infer. Weights neither set nor computed implicitly through repetition flow are zero. Task selection (Spaced Repetition Compression) prefers tasks whose implicit repetitions 'knock out other due reviews (like dominoes)' and that fend off future reviews while advancing the learner. (`observed`). Note: Every mechanism and formula was confirmed on the fetched page. Corrections: (a) the 0-to-1 weight range is implied by the 'fraction' and probability framing, not stated outright. (b) 'others are 0' leaves out that some weights are computed implicitly through repetition flow, not only set by experts. (c) The source is the designer's personal blog, not an official Math Academy page, and it is not a Brilliant source, so epistemic is observed and source_tier 2 is generous. The page also notes the post was later retitled (HSRS).
- **Detail:** Encompassing weights run from 0 to 1 (roughly, the probability that an advanced problem exercises the simpler topic). Full encompassings pass credit undiminished; partial ones let it fade. Implicit credit is discounted when it arrives early, i.e. while memory is still high. Formulas: repNum = max(0, repNum + speed*decay^failed*rawDelta); memory = max(0, memory + rawDelta)*0.5^(days/interval). decay grows with overdueness. speed = learner ability speedup divided by topic difficulty slowdown; when speed < 1 the topic ignores implicit credit and needs explicit reviews. Experts set only nontrivial weights between topics close in the graph; others are 0. Selection prefers tasks that knock out due reviews 'like dominoes', then tasks that fend off upcoming ones.

### SR-05 ★: Math Academy runs a closed loop on its knowledge graph: an adaptive diagnostic finds the knowledge frontier, lessons adapt to accuracy, review quizzes target about 80% correct, and repeated failure triggers remedial reviews of the key prerequisites, possibly several steps back in the graph.

- **Status:** `verified` · Tier 2 · <https://mathacademy.com/how-our-ai-works>
- **Detail:** The diagnostic covers about 500-1000 topics, including missing foundations, and adaptive selection cuts the number of questions by about 10x. Lessons give fewer questions at high accuracy and add questions after errors. Too many errors halt the lesson; the learner works elsewhere, then retries. A second halt with no progress triggers remedial reviews on the prerequisites exercised in that lesson. A missed quiz question triggers an immediate remedial review. Quizzes draw randomly from learned material, avoid recently quizzed topics, and adjust harder above 80% and easier below. Practice is interleaved, and related concepts are spaced apart to reduce interference.

### SR-06 ★: A production-quality prerequisite and encompassing graph has been very expensive to build by hand. Math Academy reports about 2,500 topics with 3-4 knowledge points each, each knowledge point linked to several prerequisites, and about 250 expert hours just to estimate encompassing weights.

- **Status:** `verified` · Tier 2 · <https://justinmath.com/how-math-academy-creates-its-knowledge-graph/>
- **Detail:** Encompassings for about 1,500 topics, with roughly 5 prerequisites each at about 2 minutes per estimate, took about 250 hours (about 8 hours a day for a month), done before ChatGPT. Granularity was set by expert judgment and refined over years; topics are split when problems surface. Skycak credits the hand-built graph for the task-selection model starting to work 'really, really well'. This hand-built approach cannot directly produce graphs for arbitrary topics a user requests.

### SR-07 ★: Research on scheduling skills, not facts, finds that modeling both item-to-skill links and forgetting improves prediction, and that model-based personalized review beats generic spacing in classrooms.

- **Status:** `verified` · Tier 2 · <https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.06873>
- **Detail:** DAS3H (Choffin et al., EDM 2019) notes that adaptive spacing has mostly been limited to pure memorization. It extends additive factor models with time-windowed practice history per skill and per-skill forgetting curves, and it outperforms other EDM models on 3 datasets. DASH (Lindsey, Shroyer, Pashler and Mozer, Psych. Science 2014, https://home.cs.colorado.edu/~mozer/Research/Selected%20Publications/reprints/LindseyShroyerPashlerMozer2014Published.pdf): personalized review gave 16.5% better retention than massed study and 10.0% better than one-size-fits-all spacing on a cumulative exam one month after the semester (DASH figures from search excerpts). I found no published study that combines FSRS with concept-level mastery and generated problem variants.

### KG-01 ★: Knowledge Space Theory defines the 'outer fringe' of a learner's knowledge state as the items that can be added without missing prerequisites (ready to learn), and the 'inner fringe' as the items most likely just learned. ALEKS recommends from the outer fringe.

- **Status:** `observed` · Tier 2 · <https://arxiv.org/pdf/1511.06757>
- **Detail:** Outer fringe: items x where K plus x is still a feasible state. Inner fringe: items whose removal leaves a feasible state. The Fringe Theorem says a state in a learning space is fully determined by its two fringes, so an assessment result can be summarized by them. Math Academy's 'knowledge frontier' is the same idea in practice.

### KG-02: Knowledge Space Theory has a formal procedure, QUERY, for eliciting prerequisite structure from an expert. Many answers can be inferred from earlier ones, so only a fraction of the possible questions need to be asked.

- **Status:** `observed` · Tier 2 · <https://arxiv.org/pdf/1511.06757>
- **Detail:** Queries take the form (A, q): 'if a learner fails every item in A, will they practically certainly fail q?' (Koppen 1993; Müller 1989). There are m(2^(m-1)-1) useful queries for m items, but most are inferred from earlier answers. Answers to singleton queries (r, q) build a surmise relation (a quasi-order) that encodes prerequisites (Koppen and Doignon 1990). An LLM or the user could play the expert, with transitivity used to cut the number of questions.

### KG-03 ★: A preregistered cluster-randomized trial found a large effect of interleaved math practice. A meta-analysis finds a moderate overall interleaving effect, strongest when the categories are similar and hard to tell apart.

- **Status:** `observed` · Tier 7 · <https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Study/88770>
- **Detail:** Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig and Cheung (J. Ed. Psych. 2020): 787 students, 54 classes, d=0.83 [0.68, 0.97]; an unannounced test scored 61% vs 38%. Teachers reported that mixed worksheets took longer. Brunmair and Richter (Psych. Bulletin 2019, https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf): g=0.42 across 59 studies and 238 effect sizes, substantial heterogeneity, largest for visually similar materials, small positive effect on math procedural knowledge.

### KG-04 ★: The 'Eighty Five Percent Rule' derives an optimal training error rate of about 15.87% (about 85% accuracy). It holds for gradient-descent learners on binary classification and is only suggested, not established, for human learning.

- **Status:** `observed` · Tier 2 · <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579>
- **Detail:** Wilson, Shenhav, Straccia and Cohen (Nature Communications 2019). For comparison, operational targets elsewhere are Pelánek's Outline Maps at 75% success and Math Academy quizzes at about 80%. A 75-85% predicted-correct band is a defensible default target, not a proven optimum.

### LC-01 ★: LLMs' knowledge does not follow a human-like prerequisite structure: they frequently violate knowledge dependencies, and these flaws are invisible to accuracy-based and LLM-as-judge evaluations. LLM-generated prerequisite edges therefore need external validation.

- **Status:** `verified` · Tier 2 · <https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05245>
- **Detail:** Cui, Do and Sachan (arXiv 2609.05245, Sept 2026) analyzed LLM math reasoning through Knowledge Space Theory and conclude that 'current LLMs knowledge does not follow a human-like structure'. By contrast, enumerating topics is a relative strength: Moein et al. (arXiv 2412.07422) found GPT-4 topic lists for 100+ courses more accurate (by BERTScore) than topics drawn from about 7,000 YouTube playlists.

### LC-02 ★: On a 2026 benchmark of learning-path planning over a knowledge graph, LLMs did poorly: the best model reached a 29.5% final pass rate, and none exceeded 44.7% at tailoring paths to individual learners.

- **Status:** `verified` · Tier 2 · <https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18861>
- **Detail:** PersonaPath (Liu et al., AACL-IJCNLP 2026): 2,000 learner personas and a hierarchical knowledge graph of 347 textbooks, 1,751 units and 4,092 concepts across 77 subjects. The planner must reason over learner profile, mastery state and prerequisites to choose textbook, unit and concept. Adaptivity is the main bottleneck. A search summary reported that retrieval raised validity sharply while the joint pass rate stayed low; I did not verify that on the abstract page. Implication: the LLM should propose the graph, and a deterministic algorithm should plan the path.

### LC-03 ★: One deployed system grounds personalized pathways with retrieval, validates instruction with an LLM-as-judge loop, and assesses with an expert-validated question bank. It reports large pre-to-post gains.

- **Status:** `verified` · Tier 2 · <https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-demo.50/>
- **Detail:** PathBuilder (ACL 2026 demo): 17,758 curriculum-aligned items, of which 1,018 are LLM-generated and expert-approved. 179 users (75 matched): mean absolute gain 37.9 percentage points, Hake normalized gain 0.760, Cohen's d 0.98; judge study reports a 100% threshold pass rate. The abstract mentions no control group, so the gain is pre/post (my inference). Its curriculum is fixed, not arbitrary-topic.

### LC-04 ★: OpenStax, the main open STEM textbook source for grounding generated curricula, now states that its books are CC BY-NC-SA, not CC BY. LibreTexts licensing varies page by page and can be undeclared.

- **Status:** `verified` · Tier 2 · <https://help.openstax.org/s/article/Licensing-information-of-OpenStax-textbooks>
- **Detail:** OpenStax help center: textbooks are published under CC BY-NC-SA; commercial use needs permission. The College Physics 2e preface (https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/preface) confirms CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. Some adopter copies still say CC BY 4.0, so check each book. LibreTexts 'Detailed Licensing' pages explain that remixed pages may have no single license and show as 'undeclared'; the AutoAttribution tool shows sources paragraph by paragraph. Personal local use fits NC terms; redistribution would need ShareAlike and attribution.

### LC-05 ★: Asking an LLM to review its own reasoning without external feedback does not reliably fix errors and can make answers worse. Critic passes need external signals such as solvers, retrieved text, or structural checks.

- **Status:** `verified` · Tier 2 · <https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798>
- **Detail:** Huang et al., 'Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet' (arXiv 2310.01798): in reasoning tasks, intrinsic self-correction struggles, and performance 'even degrades' in some cases. This matches Brilliant's evals approach of programmatic checks before human review.

### LC-06 ★: Claude's structured outputs use constrained decoding to guarantee that responses match a JSON schema, so generated curricula and problem configurations can be forced into the engine's types.

- **Status:** `observed` · Tier 2 · <https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/structured-outputs>
- **Detail:** JSON outputs via output_config.format with type json_schema; strict tool use (strict: true) validates tool inputs. Every object needs additionalProperties: false. SDK helpers: Pydantic via messages.parse, Zod via zodOutputFormat(). Schema validity is not semantic validity: hallucinated prerequisites and wrong answers still pass, so solver and graph checks remain necessary.

### PG-01 ★: MathCAMPS shows a sound pattern for verified generation: encode each skill standard as a formal grammar, sample symbolic problems with known answers, have an LLM write the word problem, then back-translate it to symbolic form and confirm the answer is unchanged.

- **Status:** `observed` · Tier 2 · <https://arxiv.org/html/2407.00900v1>
- **Detail:** Mishra, Poesia, Mo and Goodman (Stanford; NeurIPS 2024 workshop / COLM 2025): 44-49 K-8 Common Core standards encoded as grammars. GPT-4o turns symbolic problems into natural language, and a cycle-consistency check validates faithfulness. Symbolic structures also yield incremental and counterfactual follow-up questions. 23 LLMs evaluated; even strong models fail simple follow-ups. Grammar-generated problems also avoid fixed-benchmark contamination.

### PG-02 ★: Benchmarks check answers with a computer algebra system: simplify the difference between candidate and reference to zero, plus a numeric check that substitutes random values for the variables.

- **Status:** `observed` · Tier 2 · <https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.23851>
- **Detail:** ASyMOB (arXiv 2505.23851) uses both a symbolic check (sympy.simplify of the difference) and a numeric check (random variable substitution into both expressions). FrontierMath (arXiv 2411.04872) restricts problems to automatically checkable answers and verifies that the difference simplifies to 0 in SymPy. The PHYSICS benchmark (arXiv 2503.21821) grades with SymPy first and falls back to an LLM grader only when SymPy fails; a SciBench physics study used 5% relative tolerance for numeric answers.

### PG-03 ★: In a randomized study, LLM-generated math help produced learning gains statistically indistinguishable from human-tutor help, but only after its 32% quality-check failure rate was reduced with self-consistency.

- **Status:** `verified` · Tier 2 · <https://ideas.repec.org/a/plo/pone00/0304013.html>
- **Detail:** Pardos and Bhandari (PLOS ONE 2024, N=274, 3x4 design over algebra and statistics): ChatGPT help 'failed quality checks on 32% of problems'. Self-consistency reduced failures to nearly 0% for algebra and 13% for statistics. Only the ChatGPT condition showed significant gains over control, with no significant difference from human help in gains or time on task. The 2023 preprint covering two algebra topics found only human hints significant, so results depend on domain and safeguards.

### PG-04 ★: A Harvard RCT of an LLM physics tutor relied on prompts enriched with expert step-by-step solutions and on platform-enforced sequencing of problem parts. A system prompt alone could not scaffold multi-part problems.

- **Status:** `verified` · Tier 2 · <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/>
- **Detail:** Kestin et al. (Scientific Reports 2025, N=194, crossover design): regression effect size 0.63, which the authors call an underestimate due to ceiling; quantile regression gave 0.73-1.3 SD. Median time on task was 49 min, against about 60 min of in-class learning time. The authors 'enriched our prompts with comprehensive, step-by-step answers'. The tutor was not designed to withhold answers. Limits: one course, first exposure to material, feedback only when students asked.

### PG-05 ★: Khan Academy routes Khanmigo's numerical work through a calculator rather than the model's own predictions. It notes a separate failure mode: misjudging whether a student's answer is right even when its own math is correct.

- **Status:** `verified` · Tier 2 · <https://blog.khanacademy.org/why-were-deeply-invested-in-making-ai-better-at-math-tutoring-and-what-weve-been-up-to-lately/>
- **Detail:** Measures in the post: a calculator tool ('doing math'), upgrade to GPT-4 Turbo and testing of GPT-4o, prompting the AI to enumerate the ways a student may have reached an answer before responding, and an evaluation set of math tutoring examples to catch regressions. Dates shown on the page: May 17, 2024 and March 20, 2025.

### PG-06 ★: Raw LLM-generated multiple-choice questions have high defect rates: in one 2025 study, 49% had item-writing flaws and 22% had factual or conceptual errors, though 91% were usable starting points for revision.

- **Status:** `verified` · Tier 2 · <https://researchdiscovery.drexel.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/LLM-Generated-Multiple-Choice-Practice-Quizzes-for/991022059818004721>
- **Detail:** 'LLM-Generated Multiple Choice Practice Quizzes for Pre-Clinical Medical Students; Prevalence of Item Writing Flaws', Advances in Physiology Education 49(3), 2025; model ChatGPT, version unspecified; 59 of 65 judged reasonable starting points. Never show unverified generated items; derive the answer key and distractors from a solver or misconception model.

### PG-07 ★: Human difficulty of generated puzzles can be predicted from a solver's trace without population data. For Sudoku, a computational model of human solving reached r=0.95 with human solving time, with difficulty driven by step complexity and the dependency structure among steps.

- **Status:** `observed` · Tier 2 · <https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/flairs-sudoku.pdf>
- **Detail:** Pelánek (FLAIRS 2011): data from over 2000 puzzles; a less Sudoku-specific metric still reached r=0.88; proposed as applicable to other constraint-satisfaction problems. Uniqueness and difficulty are separate checks: a solver that counts solutions up to 2 confirms uniqueness, and a technique-based solver grades difficulty. In automatic item generation (Frontiers 2018, https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01286/pdf), 'radicals' change cognitive processing and difficulty while 'incidentals' are surface features. The Linear Logistic Test Model expresses difficulty as a weighted sum of item features, and strong-theory generation (manipulating radicals) allows difficulty prediction.

### PE-01 ★: VanLehn's 2011 review found that step-based tutoring systems (d=0.76) nearly match human tutors (d=0.79), while answer-only feedback (0.31) and finer substep-level interaction (0.40) do worse. Feedback at the level of each solution step is the sweet spot.

- **Status:** `observed` · Tier 2 · <https://quality.mit.edu/files/2012/01/Quality-Symposium-Kurt-VanLehn.pdf>
- **Detail:** Educational Psychologist 46(4), 2011. Number of effects: answer-based 165, step-based 28, substep-based 26, human 10. The review did not confirm that effectiveness keeps rising as interaction gets finer. Figures come from a reproduced table seen in search excerpts of VanLehn's talk materials.

### PE-02 ★: The realistic evidence for mastery learning and tutoring is far below Bloom's '2 sigma'. Mastery learning averages about 0.5 SD on local tests and near zero on standardized tests, and tutoring meta-analyses average about 0.33-0.37 SD.

- **Status:** `observed` · Tier 7 · <https://www.educationnext.org/two-sigma-tutoring-separating-science-fiction-from-science-fact/>
- **Detail:** Von Hippel (Education Next): Bloom's 2-sigma rests on two small 3-week dissertations in which tutored groups also got extra testing, feedback and time; the mastery group was about 1.1 SD in the original. Cohen, Kulik and Kulik 1982: about 0.33 SD; Nickow et al. 2020: 0.37 SD, with none of 96 RCTs reaching 2 sigma. Nintil review (https://nintil.com/bloom-sigma/): Kulik et al. 1990 about 0.52 overall (108 studies), about 0.08 on standardized tests; Slavin 1987 'essentially nil' on standardized measures. Kulik 1990 abstract (seen in search): effects are larger for weaker students, and self-paced mastery often reduces college completion rates.

### PE-03 ★: The Andes physics tutor gives immediate red/green feedback on each step, unsolicited messages for likely slips, and an on-demand hint sequence of pointing hint, teaching hint, then bottom-out hint. Points are deducted only for the bottom-out hint.

- **Status:** `observed` · Tier 7 · <https://arxiv.org/pdf/physics/0703224>
- **Detail:** Pointing hint: calls attention to where the error is. Teaching hint: supplies the relevant piece of knowledge. Bottom-out hint: says exactly what to change. Design principle: hints should make students derive most of the solution themselves. Students may also fix red entries without help. Related: Aleven, Roll, McLaren and Koedinger (IJAIED 2016, 'Help Helps, But Only So Much', https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/help-helps-but-only-so-much-research-on-help-seeking-with-intelli/) found that feedback on help-seeking made help use more deliberate but did not improve domain learning. The Andes primary paper sat behind an ASU access gate; the hint description is from a 2007 arXiv review.

### PE-04 ★: Problem solving before instruction (productive failure) beats instruction-first for conceptual learning in STEM. It needs some prior knowledge, works less well for young children, and depends on following up by contrasting or building on the learner's own attempts.

- **Status:** `observed` · Tier 7 · <https://futurity.org/?p=2629782>
- **Detail:** Sinha and Kapur (Review of Educational Research 2021): 53 studies, 166 comparisons in math, physics, chemistry, biology and medicine; stronger for secondary and undergraduate students than primary; problems must be intuitively graspable but need untaught concepts. Press accounts say the effect is about 2x a year of good teaching, up to 3x with high fidelity; I could not verify exact Hedges' g (paywalled). Loibl, Roll and Rummel (Ed. Psych. Review 2017, https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/towards-a-theory-of-when-and-how-problem-solving-followed-by-inst/): PS-I helps only if instruction uses contrasting cases or builds on student solutions; mechanisms are prior-knowledge activation, gap awareness and noticing deep features.

### PE-05 ★: Because of the expertise reversal effect, worked examples help novices but lose value or hurt learners with more expertise. This argues for fading examples into problems, adapted to a quick check of each learner's expertise.

- **Status:** `observed` · Tier 2 · <https://ro.uow.edu.au/edupapers/136>
- **Detail:** Sweller, Ayres, Kalyuga and Chandler (Educational Psychologist 2003). Renkl and Atkinson (2003, https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/structuring-the-transition-from-example-study-to-problem-solving--2): examples work better early, problem solving later; backward fading (remove the last step first) showed benefits. Kalyuga's rapid diagnostics (https://unsworks.unsw.edu.au/entities/publication/8382e42d-fd5f-4cb2-8063-a95b06b83499/full) use 'first-step' and 'rapid verification' tasks to estimate expertise in real time for adaptive tutorials in algebra and kinematics. A 2007 dissertation replication found null differences, so gains are not guaranteed.

### PE-06 ★: An unconstrained GPT-4 assistant raised practice scores but lowered later unaided exam scores by 17%. A tutor version with learning safeguards avoided that harm.

- **Status:** `observed` · Tier 2 · <https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html>
- **Detail:** Bastani et al. (PNAS 2025, about 1,000 high-school math students): during practice, GPT Base raised grades 48% and GPT Tutor 127%. On the unaided exam, GPT Base scored 17% lower than control and GPT Tutor about the same as control (per Wharton's summary). Students used the base model as a 'crutch'. This supports Brilliant/Koji's 'never give the answer' stance and Socratic hint ladders.

## Implications for the rebuild

- LEARNER MODEL (default): no DKT or neural knowledge tracing. Use one per-concept ability theta_c in logit units, updated online with PFAE-style Elo: theta += gamma*(1-P) on correct, theta -= delta*P on incorrect. P = g + (1-g)*logistic(theta_c - d_i), with g = 1/k for k-option MCQ and 0 for free response. Step size uses the uncertainty function U(n)=a/(1+b*n), starting at a=1, b=0.05 (Pelánek), with gamma/delta = U(n) times 1.0 and 0.5 (the asymmetric split is my inference). Keep a global per-domain ability as the prior for new concepts. Store n_attempts and last_seen so uncertainty is explicit.
- ITEM DIFFICULTY without population data: freeze each generated item's d_i from its generator features, LLTM-style: d_i = sum of weights for radicals such as solution steps, operation types, dependency depth, units or representation shifts. Weights start as expert or LLM estimates and are recalibrated slowly offline from the user's own history. Do not co-update item difficulty in the live Elo loop; Bolsinova et al. 2025 show that drifts under adaptive selection.
- MASTERY STATE (separate level from evidence): mark a concept 'provisionally mastered' when its EMA of correctness (alpha=0.85-0.9) is at least 0.85, there are 3 or more correct answers across at least 2 different surface variants, and the last answer was correct without a bottom-out hint. Promote it to 'mastered' only after a spaced, unprompted retrieval succeeds 1 or more days later. Do not use the bare 'BKT P(L)>=0.95' rule. If BKT is offered as an option, constrain P(G)<=min(0.3, 1/k+0.05) and P(S)<=0.1. All of these thresholds are inferred defaults to tune, not evidence-fixed values.
- MEMORY LAYER: one ts-fsrs (FSRS-6) card per concept or skill, not per item. Use default weights, request_retention=0.9, enable_fuzz=true. Map outcomes to ratings: Again = wrong or used the bottom-out hint; Hard = correct after pointing or teaching hints; Good = correct unaided; Easy = correct unaided and fast with the first variant. Draw every review from a fresh generated variant so the learner retrieves the skill, not a memorized item. Keep FSRS logs so reschedule() can rebuild state, and optionally run the binding optimizer after a few hundred concept reviews.
- COMBINING MASTERY WITH MEMORY: effective readiness = P_correct(theta_c, typical d) x R_fsrs(t, S_c). A concept whose retrievability falls below about 0.85 goes back into the review pool even if theta is high. A new failure on a mastered concept lowers theta and logs an FSRS lapse. Only ability (theta) feeds the knowledge-frontier unlock rule. Retrievability (R) drives review selection. This is my design inference; no published study combines FSRS with concept-level mastery.
- KNOWLEDGE GRAPH representation: a DAG of concepts. Each node has a short list of knowledge points, a generator ID set, and provenance and confidence on every edge (expert, textbook, LLM, user). Edges are typed: 'prerequisite' (hard gate) or 'encompasses' with weight w in [0,1] (credit flow), plus optional 'related or confusable' undirected edges for interleaving. Check with Kahn's algorithm on every edit, apply transitive reduction for display, and reject references to concepts that don't exist.
- PROPAGATION (FIRe-like, simplified): a successful review of X sends discounted implicit credit to encompassed prerequisites. Credit = w x (1 - R_prereq), so early credit counts little. Apply it as a fractional stability nudge outside ts-fsrs, which has no partial-review API. Failures propagate upward as small theta penalties to concepts that depend on X. Remediation goes downward: after 2 halted attempts on X, enqueue diagnostic items on X's highest-weight prerequisites, up to 2 hops back (Math Academy pattern).
- NEXT-BEST TASK SCHEDULER: candidates = outer fringe (all hard prerequisites at least provisionally mastered) plus due reviews (R < 0.9). Score = 0.4*urgency(1-R) + 0.25*compression (number of due prerequisites cleared via encompassings) + 0.25*goal relevance (on the DAG path to the user's requested target) + 0.1*interleave bonus (a confusable concept not seen in the last session) - recency penalty. Pick an item variant whose predicted P(correct) is 0.75-0.85 (Pelánek 75%, Math Academy about 80%, the theoretical 85% rule). Weights are inferred starting values.
- INTERLEAVING: introduce each new concept in a short block (2-3 items). Mixed practice sets, roughly 3-4 concepts per set, should then deliberately pair confusable concepts (e.g., series vs parallel resistance, molarity vs molality). Interleaving pays off most for hard-to-discriminate categories (g=0.42 overall; d=0.83 in the math RCT).
- LESSON SHAPE (Brilliant-like but adaptive): question-first productive-failure opener only when the learner has reached the concept through the fringe, i.e. has the prior knowledge. Follow it with instruction that explicitly contrasts the learner's attempt with the canonical solution. If a 1-item first-step diagnostic fails, or the domain is brand new to the learner, switch to worked examples with backward fading. Collapse explanations for learners who show expertise (expertise reversal).
- HINT LADDER: on each step give immediate correctness feedback and a misconception-specific message if the wrong answer matches a known error rule. On demand, offer pointing hint, then teaching hint (the principle), then bottom-out. Only the bottom-out hint counts as a failure for mastery. The AI tutor (Koji equivalent) must get the solver-verified step-by-step solution in its context (Kestin), must never reveal final answers before bottom-out (Bastani), and should list the possible student approaches before judging an answer (Khan Academy). Check numbers in its hint text with the CAS before display.
- ARBITRARY-TOPIC CURRICULUM GENERATION pipeline: (1) retrieve the table of contents and sections from local OpenStax or LibreTexts copies, checking each book's or page's license; OpenStax is CC BY-NC-SA, which suits personal local use, and attribution must be stored. (2) LLM enumerates concepts and knowledge points with structured outputs over a closed schema. (3) Judge prerequisites pairwise in both directions and discard bidirectional or low-confidence edges. (4) External critics, not self-critique: agreement with textbook chapter order, DAG check, coverage of every ToC section, and depth of 2-6 knowledge points per concept. (5) Show the user an editable graph. (6) Revise edges from data: flag possible missing prerequisites when the learner fails X despite mastering all listed prerequisites.
- DETERMINISTIC PLANNING: use the LLM only to propose graph structure and content. Path order comes from a topological sort weighted by the scheduler above, not from the LLM. The best LLM planned paths at a 29.5% pass rate in PersonaPath, and LLM prerequisite structure flaws are invisible to LLM judges.
- VERIFIED PROBLEM GENERATION (parallel to Brilliant's evals): prefer parameterized generators (strong-theory automatic item generation) whose answers come from code: SymPy for math (simplify(a-b)==0 plus random numeric substitution), a unit-aware calculator for physics and chemistry, small simulators (circuit nodal analysis, kinematics integration, stoichiometry balance) for physical plausibility, and a solver that counts solutions up to 2 for uniqueness. The LLM writes the problem text or generator config, and back-translation must reproduce the symbolic answer (MathCAMPS). Reject on any failure. Distractors come from misconception rules, not free LLM text, since about 49% of raw LLM MCQs are flawed.
- DIFFICULTY RATING: compute difficulty from the solver trace (number of steps, complexity of each operation, dependency depth), as Pelánek's Sudoku model did (r=0.95 with human solving time), and map it to a logit d_i. This lets the scheduler hit the 75-85% target band from the first session.
- EXPECTATIONS AND GATING: mastery learning's realistic effect is about 0.5 SD on local tests and near zero on standardized tests, and strict self-paced gating can reduce completion. Keep gating soft: allow test-out through a level-check that applies the same mastery criterion, never lock content behind streaks, and present an open learner model (mastery level, uncertainty, last-retrieved date per concept) to the user.

## Open questions

- Source-tier convention used here: the tier list is Brilliant-centric, so peer-reviewed papers, arXiv preprints and official library or project documentation (FSRS wiki, ts-fsrs, Anki FAQ, Claude docs, Math Academy/Skycak) are tagged tier 2. Secondary summaries, press coverage, course notes and commentary (Education Next, nintil, Futurity, quarto course notes, IES WWC entry, the arXiv review describing Andes) are tagged tier 7.
- The web search budget for this run ran out before I could check the prequestioning/pretesting-effect literature (e.g., Pan and Carpenter 2023), which would directly support Brilliant's question-first design. A follow-up search is needed.
- I found no published study combining FSRS (or any memory model) with concept-level mastery estimates and freshly generated problem variants. The combination rule in the implications is untested and should be checked locally with simulated learners.
- ts-fsrs has no API for fractional or implicit reviews. Should FIRe-style credit be done by nudging stability directly, by synthetic Hard/Good reviews with reschedule(), or by a separate custom memory model? This needs prototyping.
- The benchmark now includes FSRS-7 (34 parameters), but ts-fsrs implements FSRS-6. I did not verify whether a TypeScript FSRS-7 release exists or when one is planned.
- The Sinha and Kapur 2021 productive-failure effect sizes (Hedges' g overall and by fidelity) could not be verified because the paper is paywalled. Only press paraphrases were available.
- The BKT bounds (P(G)<0.3, P(S)<0.1) come from secondary course notes. The Corbett and Anderson 1995 and Baker et al. 2008 full texts were not retrieved.
- The Andes primary paper (VanLehn et al. 2005, IJAIED) was behind an ASU access gate. The hint-sequence details rely on a 2007 arXiv review.
- The VanLehn 2011 effect sizes (0.31/0.76/0.40/0.79) were seen only in search excerpts of a reproduced table, not in the journal article itself.
- I found no reported edge-level precision or recall for LLM-generated prerequisite edges across broad STEM topics (electronics, chemistry, biology). A local evaluation is needed, e.g., against OpenStax chapter order or a small expert-labeled graph per domain.
- Do Elo uncertainty defaults fitted on geography facts (a=1, b=0.05) transfer to multi-step STEM skills, and should a learner's ability on one concept decay between sessions or be handled entirely by the FSRS layer?
- OpenStax licensing appears to have changed (the help center now says CC BY-NC-SA, while some adopter copies still say CC BY 4.0). Should the app bundle textbook excerpts, or only store user-fetched copies locally with attribution?
- How should the engine handle topics with no open-textbook grounding (frontier or niche topics the user requests)? Options: lower confidence and require user confirmation of the graph, or ground in other licensed sources.

## Verifier summary

I checked all 12 findings against their primary sources: full PDF text for the Pelánek Elo, UMUAI and mastery papers and for the EDM 2015 poster; abstracts for Bolsinova 2025, Gervet 2020, Khajah 2016 and Pelánek & Řihák 2017; the FSRS wiki (rendered and raw); the ts-fsrs READMEs; the srs-benchmark README; the Anki FSRS FAQ; and Skycak's FIRe post. None were refuted, and the numbers, formulas and quotes were nearly all accurate. Two findings are confirmed: LM-06 (I also found a more primary source, Baker's UPenn BKT lecture slides W004V002.pdf, with the identical bounds) and LM-08 (I read the full EDM poster). The other ten are corrected. In each of them, epistemic 'verified' was applied to non-Brilliant sources, which per the rules must be 'observed'. Substantive content corrections: LM-01, 'needs no population fitting' overstates the source (difficulties and meta-parameters come from population data). LM-02, the c_ij are KC-to-KC correlations, not item-skill weights; delta's sign is not stated; and the paper warns that prerequisite-structured domains need more than these extensions. LM-07, the alpha and T ranges come from one simulated example, and EMA is distinct from the advice to separate degree of knowledge from uncertainty. SR-01, inheritance of the earlier formulas in FSRS-6 is implied, not stated. SR-02, the exact signatures of forget, reschedule and rollback are not shown in the README. SR-03, default FSRS is close on log loss but clearly worse on calibration (RMSE 0.091 vs 0.063), the default-parameter row exists only for FSRS-7, and the 0.9 retention default comes from ts-fsrs, not the FAQ. SR-04, some weights are computed implicitly, and the source is a personal blog. Design-relevant takeaways hold: start with simple models (Elo with an uncertainty function, or EMA, or N-consecutive-correct), do not co-estimate item difficulty under adaptive selection, start FSRS from defaults but optimize later, and FIRe is a credible pattern for spaced repetition over a concept graph.
